The Story

16 years of building from the inside.

My name is Daris, like Paris but with a D. I've been building businesses since 2008 across fitness, e-commerce, and coaching, and every lesson I teach comes from something I built with my own money on the line. I went from All-Conference D1 athlete to accountant to the regulation side of the NYSE to full-time entrepreneur, and I've spent the better part of two decades figuring out what it takes to build a business that actually works when the owner steps out of the room.

2005
Bucknell University, BSBA in Accounting. Born and raised in New Orleans, graduated after four years as an All-Conference Division I athlete. Hurricane Katrina changed the trajectory and brought me to New York, where I started my career on the regulation side of the New York Stock Exchange.
2008
Jersey City Fitness (JCF). Built the first boutique fitness business in Jersey City, NJ while still working full-time at the NYSE. Early mornings before the opening bell, late nights after market close, and guerrilla marketing in a city that didn't have anything like it yet.
2010
Full-time entrepreneur. Left the NYSE on March 9, 2010. I had clients, but I sat down at a Starbucks on Grove Street the next morning with no safety net and started building the business around them. That morning is the reason this newsletter launches on March 10.

The reset. Three years into running the business full-time, the model broke. Revenue was inconsistent, the business ran entirely through me, and I had no real systems to speak of. I hired a business coach, invested in learning how to sell and how to lead, and that decision reshaped every business I've built since.

COVID pivot. Moved JCF online when the world shut down and kept the community together through a year that closed most fitness businesses permanently. Ran JCF for 14 years total, 2008 through 2022.
2021
Terrell Dominick LLC. Co-founded an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife. $40K to $85K in monthly revenue, with me running operations, marketing, and manufacturing. A completely different industry built on the same systems thinking.
2026

The First 14 Days

From the Floor

Last week I ran kickoff calls with a new client, two co-founders who run a membership together. Several hundred people in their free community, a few dozen paying, and a two-week trial that hasn't been offered to the group yet.

Forty minutes in, I asked one of the founders to walk me through what a new trial member actually does in their first two days. Where they land, what they touch, where she thinks people stall out.

She had answers, and good ones. There's a Start Here page, a welcome popup, an automated email, and a steward on their team whose whole job is to send a personal message to every new member within a day or two. There's a Monday note about the week ahead and a Friday wrap-up.

That's six touches, built by two people who care a great deal about the humans on the other end, and every one of them exists because somebody noticed a need and made something to meet it. But as she listed them out loud, I could hear that the six had never been laid side by side and looked at as one thing a person moves through.

So I asked what happens between the day a trial starts and the day it ends. They track the start date, they track the end date, and they track whether the person converted. Nothing in the middle.

Then I asked about the ones who don't convert, and she told me she doesn't really know where they fall off. She told me about friends of theirs, people who love them and want this to work, who started the trial and never came back. When she followed up later, they all said a version of the same sentence. They meant to dive in, a whole stretch of time went by, and they just never did.

Their trial is 14 days long, and nobody had ever written down what those 14 days are supposed to feel like.

The System

The first 14 days of a new client are the most fragile stretch of the relationship.

That business didn't have a missing touch. It had six good ones, and the gaps between them were the problem. This is what happens when a company grows the honest way. You add what's needed when you need it, the pieces arrive one at a time over a couple of years, and the whole thing never gets laid out and looked at as one experience.

So I built theirs. Eight messages across 15 days, and the decision that shaped all of it was the size of what each one asks for.

I got that wrong on the first pass. My draft opened by asking the new member to introduce themselves, which seemed obvious to me, and the feedback came back that new people find posting intimidating and that being asked to perform on day one is what makes someone quietly close the tab. So I took the ask out entirely.

Now the first message asks for nothing. No button, on purpose. The next few ask for something private and passive, the kind of thing a person does alone in ninety seconds with nobody watching. Then something small and public, a signal rather than a performance. Then something that costs real time and puts them in front of other people. And only then, on day 12 of a 14-day trial, does anyone mention money. Six of the eight messages don't sell anything at all.

You start at zero, and each ask costs one notch more than the one before it.

It is easy to build this upside down, and I have done it. We ask for the sale upfront because it's what we want, and before we've given the customer anything. We ask for the most at the moment we have earned the least.

The bottleneck has been me. I still spend the large majority of my working hours inside Cowork, and I haven't carved out enough time to sit with Codex and actually dial in the workflows, which means my social content is lacking at the moment.

One Thing This Week

Write your nurture sequence, or update the one you have. Before you write a single subject line, list every stop in your customer's first two weeks and write down what each message asks them to do. The first should ask for nothing. Work up from there, small and private before public, and put the request to pay near the end, after they've gotten something real from you. If you're asking for money in the first few days, move it.

The first week is where your new customer decides whether you noticed they showed up. Everything you ask for after that depends on the answer.

How To Market Your Local Business Part 7: Conversion and Retention

From the Floor

At JCF we had a lot of poles in the water. Ads, referrals, events, walk-ins, and every one of them dropped leads into the business all week long. The embarrassing part was what happened next, because some weeks I followed up within the hour and some weeks a name sat for days, and our lead to trial conversion swung around depending on which version of us you caught.

It bothered me enough that I opened a spreadsheet and named it #Operationsleakyleads. Writing the names down made the problem impossible to ignore. These were real people who had raised their hand, and we were letting them cool off.

The fix was one Zapier automation with Twilio. A new lead came in, a row created in the spreadsheet, and my phone buzzed with a text carrying the lead's name, email, and mobile number. That text changed my behavior more than any goal I had ever set, because the lead was suddenly in front of me while they were still thinking about us, and I made it a rule to reach out inside the first 5 to 10 minutes.

Lead to trial conversion climbed 27 to 28% after that. Same offer, same coaches, same ads. The only thing that changed was how fast a human touched the lead.

I've built a lot of systems since then, and that one still holds the crown for return on effort. One zap, one text message, and a rule about 10 minutes.

The System

Every part of this series filled your funnel with names, and the money shows up in the two moves most owners skip, touching a new lead fast and going back to the customers you already earned.

Parts 1 through 6 built the machine that earns attention. The offer, the awareness engine, your profiles, the rooms you teach in, the paid engine, and the referral system all end the same way, with a name and a number in your hands. This final part covers what happens next, because a lead is only worth what your follow-up makes of it.

Speed decides conversion. A lead is never more interested than the moment they raise their hand, and every hour of silence after that cools them off while a competitor answers. The build has three pieces. Catch every lead in one place no matter which pole it came in on. Wire an alert so a human sees the lead the minute it lands, and a text message beats a dashboard because a text interrupts you. Then give the first touch one owner, a named person whose job is to respond inside 10 minutes with a specific next step, the trial, the estimate, the consult, whatever your front end offer from Part 1 is.

What the first touch looks like in your world.

  • Home Service: the plumber's missed call becomes a text within minutes, got your call, I can look at it Thursday morning or Friday afternoon, pick what works. The homeowner was already dialing the next name on the list, and now they stop.
  • Restaurant or Retail: the catering inquiry from the website gets a reply before lunch service ends, a menu link and two available dates, because those jobs go to whoever answers first.
  • Personal Service: the trial signup gets a personal text the same hour, a welcome by name and what to bring to the first session, so the person who was nervous about walking in feels expected.
  • Professional Service: the consult request gets a same-day call and an intake link, because someone shopping for an accountant or attorney tends to hire the first office that treats them like a client before they are one.

Retention runs on a calendar. The customer list you built across this series is the cheapest channel you own, because these people already paid you and already trust you. Retention dies at most local businesses for one simple reason, nobody scheduled it. So put three touches on an actual calendar. The post-job touch inside 48 hours, the thank you, the review ask, and the referral ask from Part 6 in one motion. The seasonal touch, whatever moment in the year naturally brings your customer back. And the 90-day win-back, a specific offer to anyone who has gone quiet, sent on a schedule instead of when you happen to think of them.

What the calendar looks like in your world.

  • Home Service: the HVAC company texts last spring's tune-up customers in March, before the first hot week, and books days of work from names it already had.
  • Restaurant or Retail: the boutique keeps a list of its best 50 customers and texts them the night before new stock hits the floor, so the people most likely to buy get first pick.
  • Personal Service: the studio sends a 90-day win-back to lapsed members with a specific comeback offer and a date it expires, and some of those members just needed to be invited back.
  • Professional Service: the accountant runs a quarterly check-in across the whole client base, so when a client's situation changes the first call goes to them and the book renews on relationship.

How To Market Your Local Business Part 6: Referrals and Partnerships

From the Floor

For years I treated referrals like a tip, something a happy member might leave on the way out if I was lucky and remembered to ask. Then I stepped into running JCF as an owner instead of the trainer, and I changed the rule. Referrals became a condition of doing business with us.

It reads more aggressive on paper than it ever felt in the room. Most of our members started on a trial, so by the time I closed them on a program they had already felt what we did, and the ask came right there at the table. After we finalized the new member's annual membership, I'd let them know, we are going to get you results, that part is on us, and in return I'd like to work with more people like you. Most of them had two or three in mind as soon as I finished the sentence.

We backed it up in the welcome packet. Every new member got a folder, a little swag, and a referral letter, and that letter turned into a referral card where they wrote down a friend's name, mobile number, and email so we could follow up ourselves instead of hoping they would pass our flyer along. We were not shy about asking for the favor, because we had a track record of doing what we said, and that earned us the right to ask.

Running it that way taught me something I still coach. When someone commits to you and you feel the weight of getting them a result, asking for a referral feels like part of the same handshake. The people who already trust you are the cheapest growth you will ever find, so build the referral ask into your sales or retention system, and stop leaving money on the table.

The System

The cheapest customers you will ever get come from the people who already trust you, when you build the ask into the business instead of leaving it to luck.

Part 5 leaned on paid ads to buy attention. This week we borrow trust, the channels where someone else's relationship does the selling for you. Two of them carry the most weight for a local business, referrals and partnerships, and they grow on goodwill instead of ad spend. Referrals are the one I would build first, because the trust is already yours and the only thing missing is the system.

Make referrals a condition of doing business. The mistake is waiting until someone is walking out the door, when the moment of highest trust is the moment they say yes to you. Ask at the close, while they are committed and a little proud of the decision they just made. Keep the ask specific, names of people they have already mentioned wanting what you sell, because specific is what their brain can actually answer. Make passing it along about a ten-second job, a card to fill out or a text you send them to forward, so the easiest thing for them to do is the thing you want.

Build it into something they hold, then follow up yourself. A referral that lives in your customer's good intentions never shows up. Put it in their hands the way we did, a welcome packet with a card where they write down a name, a mobile number, and an email, and then you make the call or the text. Owning the follow-up is the whole difference, because it moves the work off their memory and onto your system, and your system shows up every time.

Run a few overt plays on a calendar. Beyond the one-on-one ask, build moments designed to bring friends through the door. At JCF we ran themed workouts with a DJ on a Friday evening, a slot we did not normally use, and called it a happy hour, and members brought people who would never have walked in cold. We ran referral contests, free-week competitions, and socials, and we captured contact information at every one of them, then put a small gift or a raffle behind the referral so the person doing the referring had a reason to act now. Pick one of these a quarter and the borrowed-trust channel turns from a someday idea into something on your calendar.

What this looks like in your world.

  • Home Service: a plumber or HVAC tech asks at the end of the job, when the customer is happiest because the problem is gone, and hands over a card or texts a link, who else on your street has a water heater this old, so the warm intro happens while the relief is fresh.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a boutique runs a bring-a-friend night and gives both the customer and the friend a small gift, and at checkout the owner captures the friend's name and number so the relationship does not end when they leave the store.
  • Personal Service: a salon or studio asks at the result moment, right after the color or the goal that got hit, and sends the client home with a guest pass for a friend that has the client's name on it so the front desk knows who to thank.
  • Professional Service: an attorney, accountant, or advisor asks at the win, the case closed or the refund that landed, and because their best referrals come through other professionals, they keep a short list of people they trust to send work back and forth with on purpose.

Partnerships put your name in front of someone else's customers. A partnership is two local businesses who share a customer and do not compete, agreeing to send value to each other's people. The best one we ran at JCF was the Rockstar program. Every week the coaches voted on the member who stood out, that member signed our Rockstars board, which now hangs as a wall of member autographs, and they walked away with a reward our partners paid for, 25% off House of Gains, the local meal-prep delivery, and 30% off New Jersey Running Company in Hoboken, which later became JackRabbit. The member felt celebrated, the partners got warm customers who already trusted our word, and we got a recognition program we did not have to fund ourselves. The trust ran in every direction at once.

Your version starts by listing the businesses that already serve your customer right before or right after you do, then building one thing that sends value their way.

  • Home Service: a landscaper and a pool company hand each other the job that sits just outside their own lane, so the customer gets taken care of and neither one chases a lead they were never going to win.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a cafe and the boutique next door run a show-your-receipt discount, and each storefront quietly feeds the other foot traffic all week.
  • Personal Service: a studio and a meal-prep company do what we did, fund each other's customer rewards so both brands ride along on a perk neither paid for alone.
  • Professional Service: an accountant and an attorney send the work that is not theirs to do, and the referral partner becomes the steadiest source of new clients either one has, which is why professionals live or die on these relationships.

One thing to know. The reason most referral programs die is that the ask lives in the owner's head, so it happens when they remember and it stops when they get busy. This workbook moves it onto paper and into your onboarding, so it runs whether you are having a good week or not.

How To Market Your Local Business Part 5: The Local Digital Engine

From the Floor

My wife was shooting new looks every week for terrelldominick.com, so we took 45 of them, sorted them into categories like elevated athleisure, chic neutrals, pops of color, and denim on denim, and turned the whole thing into a free download we called Top 45 Looks for Stylish Moms.

We ran it cold on Meta to women who had never heard of us, and we built the audience by carving out everyone who already knew the brand, past visitors, our email list, anyone who had followed the page or watched a video, so the budget only ever chased strangers. The objective was leads instead of sales, which kept the clicks cheap, and the book pulled in 3,789 new emails and phone numbers. There was a discount code at the end of it, and that one code brought in enough orders to basically cover the ad spend, so we were growing the list for close to free. That campaign turned $4,634 in spend into $10,408 in sales, a little better than 2x, and a separate ad for our best-selling Balloon Top came back over 6x.

For a long time I thought a strong ad was the whole game. What actually moved the numbers was the order we put things in. We met strangers with something free, we followed the ones who hadn't bought with a reason to act, and we kept the people who had already bought close with new looks and no discount. Top of funnel, middle of funnel, bottom of funnel. That same ladder works whether you sell clothes online or fill a calendar in your town, and it is the entire job of your local digital engine.

The System

Paid ads work when you match the message to the temperature.

Part 2 made you familiar and Part 4 turned a warm room into customers, and paid ads speed all of it up by putting your offer in front of the right locals on purpose. Most of us start by running one ad to everyone, when the move is to build for three stages and let each one carry its own message.

Top of funnel is strangers, so grow the pie. These are locals who have never heard of you. Target them by location first, a pin on your shop with a radius that fits your market, 3 to 5 miles in a dense city like Jersey City and 5 to 10 across most of the country, or the zip codes you serve. Keep the rest of your targeting broad, lead with something free or low-risk like a guide or a first-visit offer, and let the creative sell the outcome they actually want. Set the objective to conversions almost every time. A lead is the cheaper conversion to chase and a purchase costs more, so at the top you start by getting leads, and if you have budget to test traffic or engagement you can, though conversions is the objective that earns its keep for a small business.

Middle of funnel is the people who looked, engaged, and didn't buy. They visited your site, watched a video, opened your form, or followed you, and then went quiet. Build an audience out of exactly those people and put a reason to act in front of them, a limited offer or the next piece of the teaching. This is the stage I see skipped most, and it is usually the easiest to win, because these people already know you and just need a reason to come back.

Bottom of funnel is your customers. Upload your customer list and put your offers back in front of the people who have already paid you. You can drop the discount here, because they already trust you, and this is where the message gets to grow up. Up top you lead with the practical reason to buy and a deal to get a stranger over the hump, and with your customers you can speak to the bigger why, the ease it gives them, the pride they feel, the way it changes how they move through their day, and the standing it earns them with the people they care about. Run these as retargeting ads on a short window, around 60 days, so your spend stays on customers while their last visit is still fresh.

The ad sends people two places, your offer and your profile. A good chunk of locals will tap your Instagram or Facebook before they ever click your offer, just to see if you are real, active, and serving people like them. If your last post is from eight months ago, the ad money leaks right there. When I ran JCF, our social was all community and results, the faces and the before-and-afters, because people will quit a gym long before they quit the friends they made inside it. Keep your profile alive with recent work and real results so the curious click lands somewhere that closes the sale instead of raising a doubt.

One honest note on Google. I run Meta for local because that is where I have spent the money and watched it return. Your Google reach comes free from the Google Business Profile you tightened up in Part 3, which puts you in front of people already searching for what you do, so let Meta carry the paid work and let that profile carry search.

From the Floor

Early on, before JCF had a dollar for ads, I got lucky. One of my Members was married to a guy named David LeLong, and David built websites and ran SEO for a living. That came up more times than I can count, and it was usually the nudge that moved someone from curious to standing in front of me. The System

Awareness makes a stranger curious, and your profile is where that curiosity either turns into a customer or quietly leaks away.

Part 2 was about becoming familiar before someone needs you. This week is the next beat, the moment a warm prospect goes looking. They saw your shirt, your sign, or your booth, they want what you sell, and they search your name or your service plus your town. What they find in the next ten seconds decides whether they call you or call someone else.

Findable beats clever. When warm turns into searching, the whole game is whether you show up and whether the information is right. A prospect who can't find your hours, hits a wrong address, or sees a half-built profile moves to the next result, and the awareness work from Part 2 leaks out through that broken listing.

Completeness and consistency win. Your name, address, and phone have to match everywhere they appear, across Google, Apple Maps, your website, Yelp, and your industry directories. When the details conflict, search engines and customers both trust you less, and you slide down the results. Filling a profile out all the way, with real hours, photos, services, and a clear way to call or book, does more for most owners than another round of ads.

Findability has two layers now. The listing layer tells a stranger you are real, where you are, and that you are legit, led by Google Business Profile with Apple Maps, your main review site, Bing, and your industry directories behind it. The social-search layer is where people open Instagram or TikTok to see whether you are current before they commit, and for a lot of buyers it's Google first and your Instagram second, where a polished profile next to a dead Instagram reads as a warning. How much each layer carries depends on what you do, so plant your flag here first.

  • Home Service Business: the listing layer does almost all the work. Google Business Profile is the anchor, plus trade platforms like Angi, Thumbtack, and Nextdoor, and a service-area setting so you show up for the towns you cover. Social is light, a few job photos that prove the work.
  • Restaurant or Retail: Google and Apple Maps for the pin and hours, plus Yelp and your delivery or reservation platforms. Instagram pulls real weight, because people check your grid for current dishes, the room, and whether the place looks alive.
  • Personal Service Business: here the social layer can rival Google. A gym, a barber, a stylist, or a tattoo artist gets judged by eye, so Instagram and TikTok sit right next to your Google profile and your booking tool, showing the space and real people in it.
  • Professional Service Business: Google plus the credential directories that signal trust, like Avvo, Healthgrades, Zillow, and your association listings. The directory doubles as a credibility check, and LinkedIn does the social-proof job Instagram does for the visual trades.

Reviews are the tiebreaker. Once a prospect finds two or three options that all look capable, reviews decide it, and the count, the recency, and your replies all matter. A steady stream of recent reviews tells a stranger that real people keep choosing you, and a thoughtful reply to a hard one shows how you handle a problem, which is often what they're really worried about. Asking every happy customer right when they're happiest is the highest-leverage small habit in this issue.

The trap is set-and-forget. Most owners claim a Google profile once, fill in half of it, and never look again. The hours go stale, the photos age, the number changes, and a competitor with a fresher listing quietly takes the calls. Profiles are a living layer that needs tending.

The build order. Claim and complete your Google Business Profile first, since that's where the search moment happens for most local businesses. Match your name, address, and phone everywhere. Add real photos and a clear booking or call step. Start a simple habit for collecting reviews. Then add the one or two industry directories that matter, and tend the social profiles people use to validate you, heavier if you're a visual trade. One platform done all the way beats five done halfway.

How To Market Your Local Business Part 2: The Awareness Engine

From the Floor

I didn't understand frameworks back then, and I didn't realize what I was building.

We were working out on the waterfront at Exchange Place, and we created JCF gear that we liked and wanted to wear daily. We designed flyers and placed them in local businesses, and we signed an agreement with JCDecaux for billboards in the Jersey City PATH train stations. We placed ads in local magazines like JC Independent and NEW Magazine. We had a booth at every local event we could get into, we posted yard signs from time to time, we gave out free merch at events, and our Members earned JCF gear by hitting milestones.

The JCF merch flooded downtown JC, and you'd see JCF gear somewhere in your day, on a coffee run, on the PATH, in the grocery store. Members and team walking around in branded gear became our billboards.

Working out outdoors in high-foot-traffic locations meant new prospects could see the army of JCFers in motion. They saw the camaraderie, the professional coaching, the intensity, and people leaving with smiles and chatting with each other. They saw people who looked like them performing exercises they used to do or felt they should be doing.

It inevitably led to one question.

What is this?

We sponsored local community events too. Most times a Member or Team Member had an organization they wanted to support, and we sponsored it.

None of it felt like a huge deal on its own. Looking back, each of these pieces (minus a vehicle wrap, which we never had) stacked into the awareness engine of JCF, my local fitness business. We layered local Facebook ads on later, and the combination made JCF hard to ignore.

Local marketing isn't only about getting someone to buy today. It's about becoming familiar before they need you, so when it's time, you become the only logical choice. The static awareness engine turns cold locals into warm locals and keeps your lead funnel full.

The System

Static awareness makes strangers feel familiar with your business before they ever click, call, or walk in.

Most local business owners wait until someone is ready to buy before they try to be remembered. The best local owners become familiar first, and the static awareness engine is how they do it. The work feels small in the moment but stacked over time, it's the difference between being a name people consider and being the name they already trust.

The Local Funnel. Every local audience moves through three stages, and your job is to make the move easier at every step.

  • Cold: Someone sees your sign, your shirt, your sponsorship, or your booth for the first time, and your name is new to them.
  • Warm: They've seen you a few times, the name is starting to stick, and they could pull it out of memory if a friend asked who to call.
  • Hot: They need the service, your name comes up first, and they call, visit, book, or buy.

Static channels help turn cold locals into warm ones. They are the steady layer of marketing that builds recognition over time, so every ad, post, email, or offer works better.

Two layers, one engine. Local marketing has two layers.

Static is the steady presence people see again and again. Signage, shirts, merch, packaging, sponsorships, community visibility, and print in the right local places. It builds recognition over time without needing a new campaign every week.

Dynamic is the active push. Meta ads, Google ads, social posts, email, retargeting, and PR. It helps move people from interested to ready.

Strong local businesses use both. Static makes people familiar with you. Dynamic gives them a reason to act. When you skip static, your ads have to introduce you and sell you at the same time. When static is working, your ads are landing in front of people who already feel like they know you.

Issue 017 goes deeper on dynamic marketing. This issue is about the static foundation.

A note on examples. Going forward in this series, every example groups into four categories so you can spot yours quickly: a) Home Service Business, b) Restaurant or Retail, c) Personal Service Business, and d) Professional Service Business.

The six static channels.

1. Signage. Storefront signs, branded merch, yard signs, A-frame signs, window decals, stickers, and vehicle wraps or magnets. Funnel job: cold people notice you, and warm people see proof you work nearby.

  • Home Service Business: a truck wrap that turns every job-site arrival into a moving billboard, plus a yard sign at every completed job.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a clean storefront sign that reads from the sidewalk, plus an A-frame on the corner Tuesday through Sunday.
  • Personal Service Business: a window decal at the studio, plus car magnets and a gym-bag patch at the local rec center if you're mobile.
  • Professional Service Business: office suite signage, branded leather portfolios, magnetic vehicle logo.

2. Branded uniforms, shirts, and merch for owner, team, and customers. Polos, jackets, hats, and gear that the team wears on the job and that customers wear after the fact. Funnel job: warm people feel you're professional, and your customers and team become walking signage that puts cold people in front of your name.

  • Home Service Business: an HVAC team in matching branded polos that read as professional on the first home visit.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a branded ball cap that regulars wear around town and a staff uniform that's photo-ready.
  • Personal Service Business: branded shirts on stylists or trainers, plus client gear earned through milestones the way JCF Members did.
  • Professional Service Business: less uniform-driven, with a branded blazer pin and tote and a branded executive portfolio.

3. Packaging, bags, and take-home materials. Branded bags, boxes, stickers on packaging, business cards in the bag, take-home guides, branded napkins, anything the customer carries out the door with your name on it. Funnel job: customers remember and share you, and the bag travels in front of cold eyes long after the transaction.

  • Home Service Business: a branded folder left at the end of every job with the warranty, the next-service reminder, and a referral card.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a branded takeout bag that rides the bus across town at lunchtime.
  • Personal Service Business: a branded gift bag at the first visit with the booking link and a friend-referral card inside.
  • Professional Service Business: welcome packets and onboarding binders, and a leave-behind packet with engagement materials.

4. Local sponsorships. Little league teams, school yearbook ads, charity 5Ks, civic events, community theater programs, and school fundraisers. Funnel job: warm people connect you with the community, and your name shows up next to causes the audience already cares about.

  • Home Service Business: sponsoring a youth baseball team with your logo on the jersey.
  • Restaurant or Retail: sponsoring the local high school's fall play program with a quarter-page in the playbill.
  • Personal Service Business: sponsoring a community 5K with a finish-line table and free water.
  • Professional Service Business: bar association dinners, chamber of commerce, small business expos, nonprofit galas. The sponsorship is twofold, visibility and credibility.

5. Community visibility. Booths at farmer's markets, parades, festivals, neighborhood association meetings, civic boards, school career days, and ribbon cuttings. Outdoor presence in high-foot-traffic spots when it fits your business, the way the JCF waterfront workouts did. Funnel job: cold people meet you face-to-face, which is the fastest cold-to-warm channel there is.

  • Home Service Business: a landscaper at the spring home-and-garden expo with sample plant boards and quote slips.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a cafe pouring coffee at the Saturday farmer's market with a takeout flyer at the table.
  • Personal Service Business: a fitness coach running a 20-minute mobility class at the community center, or a salon doing braid bars at a community festival.
  • Professional Service Business: chamber boards, free monthly workshops, civic boards, speaking at industry events.

6. Print presence in the right local publications. Print ads, sponsored features, regular columns, billboards, neighborhood mailers, community magazines, church bulletins, and school newsletters. Funnel job: cold people notice you, and the repetition over months turns notice into recognition.

  • Home Service Business: a monthly quarter-page in the regional homeowner magazine.
  • Restaurant or Retail: a billboard on the route home from the office park, plus a quarterly feature in the neighborhood lifestyle publication.
  • Personal Service Business: a quarterly column in the community wellness magazine, plus an ad in the school yearbook every spring.
  • Professional Service Business: billboards, and authored columns in the local business journal.

The trap. Most owners focus on the marketing they can measure: clicks, views, leads, and sales. So they spend on ads and content, but ignore the things people see in real life every day. The problem is that every campaign has to work harder when nobody knows you yet. Your ad has to introduce you, build trust, and get someone to act all at once. Static marketing makes that job easier. It helps people recognize your name before the ads ever run.

The build order. Start with the highest-frequency, lowest-cost static channel for your business and audience. For a brick-and-mortar, that's maybe signage and packaging. For a home service business with vehicles, it's the wrap and the yard signs. For a community-rooted personal service business, it's local sponsorships and community visibility. Pick one, build it right, and add the next.

Both converted into the annual membership at multiples of what cold outreach to the membership ever did. By the time I left the fitness industry, the architecture was second nature.

That's why what happened at Smart Marketer Live in Denver landed the way it did.

I was a few years into running an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife, still working with a smaller roster of hybrid JCF clients through my training app on the side. Our paid ads were running at a 3 to 4:1 ROAS, which by every standard is healthy. I sat down for lunch on day two of the conference with Taylor, a paid ads strategist I'd met that morning, and I was walking him through what we were doing. He listened, then he asked me one question.

"Are you running your top-of-funnel ads to your full catalog, or just to one hero product?"

Top of funnel is the term for ads aimed at people who don't know your brand yet. Cold audiences. The ads that have to do the introducing.

We were doing what most e-commerce brands do, which was running New Arrivals ads to top of funnel and letting the catalog do the work. He was telling me to flip it. Pick the one product you trust the most, the one with the cleanest creative and the strongest organic engagement and the most consistent reviews, and run only that to your cold audiences. Use the rest of the catalog for retargeting and email.

That's when the full circle hit me. The hero product was acting as the FEO, with the rest of the catalog playing the role of the core. I'd been running this exact strategy in fitness for over a decade, and now I was watching the same model show up in e-commerce.

I sat on the conversation for a few months because we didn't have a finished hero product yet. The next big drop was still in production. When it landed in the warehouse, we created social media content and posted asap. Over the following few days, we built paid ads around the creatives that were already winning organically on social, pointed them all at the new hero, and pressed go.

The next two months were the highest-grossing months that business has ever had. We sold out of the product nine times. A couple thousand units moved. We ran that exact playbook for nearly a year with one piece, the Denim Balloon Top, and the success drew copycats. My wife's content started showing up on competitor sites without permission. That's a different problem for a different issue.

What the front-end-to-core architecture does for a local business is the same thing the hero-product-to-catalog architecture did for our e-commerce brand. It puts an offer matched to where the customer actually is, and the math takes over from there.

The System

Local businesses need two offers, not one.

Most owners I work with sell a single offer. The core thing they do. The long engagement, the annual contract, the full project, the ongoing patient care, the membership. It's the offer that pays the rent and grows the business.

The problem is they market that offer to people who have never heard of them before. Strangers don't buy a $2,500 annual membership from a gym they're just hearing about through a Google search, they don't sign an annual HVAC maintenance contract from a flyer in their mailbox, and they don't commit to a 12-month patient plan from a dentist they haven't met. They might think about it. They don't buy.

That's where the front-end offer comes in.

A front-end offer is a low-friction, time-bounded first step designed for one job, which is turning a stranger into a customer. It's not the big offer or the money offer. It's the offer that earns you the right to make the money offer later, after the person has actually experienced what you do.

Here's what a good front-end offer looks like.

It promises a specific outcome inside a short time window. A $99 HVAC tune-up that ends with a written system report. A $59 chef's tasting menu for two on a quiet Tuesday. A free in-home design consult that ends with a stage-by-stage plan and a ballpark number. A $49 new-patient assessment that ends with a clear care recommendation. The customer walks out with something concrete, fast.

The price clears the friction. That doesn't mean cheap. There are two paths to a price that clears friction, and both work depending on the audience.

The first is a low absolute number. A $29 chef's tasting menu, a $99 HVAC tune-up, a $49 chiropractic assessment. Small dollar, small decision, the buyer can say yes without checking with anyone.

The second is a higher price paired with so much packed value that the cost-to-value ratio is obvious from the first sentence of the offer. The Weight-Loss Challenges I ran at JCF were the value-stacked kind, 6 to 8 weeks at $280 to $500. Every challenge included a nutrition program, group accountability, pre and post-program fitness evaluations, and the workouts JCF was known for. The price felt small relative to what someone was actually getting, and they converted at multiples of what a smaller-dollar entry point would have done.

Both paths work. The trap is pricing in the middle, a number too high to feel like a small decision and too low to signal premium value. That middle is where most FEOs go to die.

The cheap-and-fast path fits a high-volume, broad-reach audience, and the high-value-at-premium-price path fits a niche, high-intent audience that already understands what this kind of work normally costs. Pick the path that fits where your customer actually lives.

The other layer most owners miss is the upsell at the moment of transaction. Even when the FEO itself isn't profitable on the way in, the transaction is still a chance to build margin. A restaurant runs a $29 Couples Tasting and upsells a $35 wine-and-dessert package when the guests are seated. An HVAC company runs a $99 tune-up and has a written "while we're here" list of small fixes priced for same-day approval. A chiropractor runs a $49 assessment and offers a 3-pack of adjustments at the end of the appointment. The FEO clears the friction at the door, and the upsell at the moment of transaction is often where the margin lives.

It has a real deadline. "Book by Friday for our spring slot." "Limited to 20 new patients this month." "Available through May 30." The deadline is what turns "I should look into that someday" into "I need to call them this week." Most front-end offers fail because the door stays open forever and there's no reason to walk through it now.

It's a slice of the core, not a different thing. This is the part most owners get wrong. They build a front-end offer that doesn't naturally lead into anything else. A coupon for a free dessert at a restaurant doesn't lead anywhere except to a discount habit. A free coffee mug for HVAC customers doesn't lead anywhere except to a closet full of mugs. The front-end has to be a real preview of the core service so the natural next step is the core engagement itself.

Here's the math that usually convinces local owners to actually build one.

Run 100 cold leads at your core offer. Two convert if you're lucky, and even those two took multiple touches and a long sales cycle to get there. Run those same 100 cold leads at a well-designed front-end offer and 15 to 25 will convert. Of those 15 to 25 customers, somewhere between 40 and 60 percent move into the core offer over the following 90 days. Same lead pool, six to ten times the revenue, faster.

The mistake most owners make is trying to make money on the front-end itself. The front-end isn't supposed to be profitable. It's the cost of acquiring a customer who will be profitable over the next twelve months. Once you internalize that, the price stops being a problem and starts being a tool.

Here's how the same architecture shows up across different local businesses.

Restaurant. Front-end: a $59 chef's tasting menu for two, or a $45 wine pairing class on a slow Tuesday. Core: regular dinner traffic, private events, catering contracts, and birthday-club memberships.

HVAC. Front-end: a $99 seasonal system tune-up with a written report. Core: the $400 annual maintenance contract, plus the replacement systems that come with the relationship over time.

Home builder / custom cabinet shop. Front-end: a free in-home design consult with a written scope and a ballpark number. Core: the full build or installation.

Dentist. Front-end: a $99 new-patient exam plus cleaning plus whitening. Core: the ongoing patient relationship.

Real estate agent. Front-end: a free market analysis or buyer consultation with a written 90-day plan. Core: the listing or buyer engagement.

Chiropractor. Front-end: a $49 assessment plus first adjustment. Core: the multi-month treatment plan.

Same architecture, different package on the front. Every one of these owners can build a front-end version of their business in the next 30 days, and once it's running, the same marketing dollars convert at multiples of what they were.

Build the front-end first. Then we'll talk about how to drive traffic to it.

Three C's of Hiring

From the Floor

Late 2017, Melissa S. left her admin role at JCF. My wife had filled in during past gaps in that seat, and with multiple young kids at home she couldn't cover this one.

By that point I had built something I'm still grateful for. Many of my hires came from inside the JCF community, members of the program who already understood the culture, and that was on purpose. Yari, then Melissa S., then a long stretch of solid coverage in member relations and admin. Each of them carried JCF the way the brand deserved to be carried. The hiring system around them was real, with personality tests, multiple rounds of interviews, and a probationary period. We had collaborated to write the JCF Playbook in Google Sites, and the playbook covered most of what the role needed to do day to day. From the outside, the hiring machine looked dialed.

Inside, I was overwhelmed. The seat had been open too long, and I needed someone on the floor of the office handling the steady, unglamorous admin work yesterday. I ran the candidates through the process and it came down to three. The hire I made was someone I knew. Our kids went to school together. She was funny and warm and fit the JCF crowd. I liked her and trusted her, so I hired her.

She didn't last. The admin tasks were the main reason the seat existed, and the work was inconsistent and the call-outs piled up. Several months after her probationary period ended it was clear the role wasn't right for her, and that became the only fire I made at JCF.

Almost a decade later, what has stayed with me is that the failure was above her. I had built a real hiring process and walked it past two of my own rules to make a hire that fit the moment. I had hired in a state of overwhelm because the seat had been empty too long, and I had hired without being clear on what JCF the brand actually needed in that chair, which was the steady administrative consistency of running the same systems day after day. I had hired for the wrong job.

The System

Run the three Cs before you post the job.

When the structure is consistent, the personality on top of it has room to breathe. When the structure is missing, the personality is doing all the work, and the experience depends entirely on which team member shows up that day.

The Anatomy of a JCF Session didn't tell coaches what to say or how to coach. It told them what the session looks like, the order of events, the bookends, the non-negotiable touchpoints. Within that structure, every coach brought their own style and their own energy, and the members got the benefit of both: a consistent experience and a human one.

The question worth sitting with is not "is my team doing it wrong." The question is "have I defined what the experience should feel like in a way that doesn't depend on me being in the room."

If the answer is no, your team is operating on discretion. And discretion, across a team, produces exactly what you're seeing: inconsistency that feels like a people problem but is actually an infrastructure problem.

The standard doesn't belong to you. It belongs to the company. When you build it that way, the company can hold it whether you're there or not.

Pick one client-facing experience your team delivers regularly.
