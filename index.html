The Story

16 years of building from the inside.

My name is Daris, like Paris but with a D. I've been building businesses since 2008 across fitness, e-commerce, and coaching, and every lesson I teach comes from something I built with my own money on the line. I went from All-Conference D1 athlete to accountant to the regulation side of the NYSE to full-time entrepreneur, and I've spent the better part of two decades figuring out what it takes to build a business that actually works when the owner steps out of the room.

2005
Bucknell University, BSBA in Accounting. Born and raised in New Orleans, graduated after four years as an All-Conference Division I athlete. Hurricane Katrina changed the trajectory and brought me to New York, where I started my career on the regulation side of the New York Stock Exchange.
2008
Jersey City Fitness (JCF). Built the first boutique fitness business in Jersey City, NJ while still working full-time at the NYSE. Early mornings before the opening bell, late nights after market close, and guerrilla marketing in a city that didn't have anything like it yet.
2010
Full-time entrepreneur. Left the NYSE on March 9, 2010. I had clients, but I sat down at a Starbucks on Grove Street the next morning with no safety net and started building the business around them. That morning is the reason this newsletter launches on March 10.
2013
The reset. Three years into running the business full-time, the model broke. Revenue was inconsistent, the business ran entirely through me, and I had no real systems to speak of. I hired a business coach, invested in learning how to sell and how to lead, and that decision reshaped every business I've built since.
2020
COVID pivot. Moved JCF online when the world shut down and kept the community together through a year that closed most fitness businesses permanently. Ran JCF for 14 years total, 2008 through 2022.
2021
Terrell Dominick LLC. Co-founded an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife. $40K to $85K in monthly revenue, with me running operations, marketing, and manufacturing. A completely different industry built on the same systems thinking.
2026
From The Floor Up + The Staged CEO™. Launched a weekly newsletter for service business owners and started building The Staged CEO Accelerator, a 12-week program for home staging entrepreneurs who are ready to stop doing the work and start running the company. The work continues.

How To Market Your Local Business Part 1: The Offer Architecture

From the Floor

Front-end offers as a model first showed up in my world through Bedros Keuilian. I joined his mastermind in the JCF years, and the concept hit a different gear when I went through Alex Hormozi's Gym Launch program, where FEOs sat at the center of how the whole business ran. I ran two types of FEO at JCF for a long stretch. The 21-day and 28-day programs were the price-friction kind, designed to give first-timers a low-cost test drive of the program. The Weight-Loss Challenges were the value-stacked kind, 6 to 8 weeks at $280 to $500, built to deliver a real body transformation that justified the price. Both converted into the annual membership at multiples of what cold outreach to the membership ever did. By the time I left the fitness industry, the architecture was second nature.

That's why what happened at Smart Marketer Live in Denver landed the way it did.

I was a few years into running an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife, still working with a smaller roster of hybrid JCF clients through my training app on the side. Our paid ads were running at a 3 to 4:1 ROAS, which by every standard is healthy. I sat down for lunch on day two of the conference with Taylor, a paid ads strategist I'd met that morning, and I was walking him through what we were doing. He listened, then he asked me one question.

"Are you running your top-of-funnel ads to your full catalog, or just to one hero product?"

Top of funnel is the term for ads aimed at people who don't know your brand yet. Cold audiences. The ads that have to do the introducing.

We were doing what most e-commerce brands do, which was running New Arrivals ads to top of funnel and letting the catalog do the work. He was telling me to flip it. Pick the one product you trust the most, the one with the cleanest creative and the strongest organic engagement and the most consistent reviews, and run only that to your cold audiences. Use the rest of the catalog for retargeting and email.

That's when the full circle hit me. The hero product was acting as the FEO, with the rest of the catalog playing the role of the core. I'd been running this exact strategy in fitness for over a decade, and now I was watching the same model show up in e-commerce.

I sat on the conversation for a few months because we didn't have a finished hero product yet. The next big drop was still in production. When it landed in the warehouse, we created social media content and posted asap. Over the following few days, we built paid ads around the creatives that were already winning organically on social, pointed them all at the new hero, and pressed go.

The next two months were the highest-grossing months that business has ever had. We sold out of the product nine times. A couple thousand units moved. We ran that exact playbook for nearly a year with one piece, the Denim Balloon Top, and the success drew copycats. My wife's content started showing up on competitor sites without permission. That's a different problem for a different issue.

What the front-end-to-core architecture does for a local business is the same thing the hero-product-to-catalog architecture did for our e-commerce brand. It puts an offer matched to where the customer actually is, and the math takes over from there.

The System

Local businesses need two offers, not one.

Most owners I work with sell a single offer. The core thing they do. The long engagement, the annual contract, the full project, the ongoing patient care, the membership. It's the offer that pays the rent and grows the business.

The problem is they market that offer to people who have never heard of them before. Strangers don't buy a $2,500 annual membership from a gym they're just hearing about through a Google search, they don't sign an annual HVAC maintenance contract from a flyer in their mailbox, and they don't commit to a 12-month patient plan from a dentist they haven't met. They might think about it. They don't buy.

That's where the front-end offer comes in.

A front-end offer is a low-friction, time-bounded first step designed for one job, which is turning a stranger into a customer. It's not the big offer or the money offer. It's the offer that earns you the right to make the money offer later, after the person has actually experienced what you do.

Here's what a good front-end offer looks like.

It promises a specific outcome inside a short time window. A $99 HVAC tune-up that ends with a written system report. A $59 chef's tasting menu for two on a quiet Tuesday. A free in-home design consult that ends with a stage-by-stage plan and a ballpark number. A $49 new-patient assessment that ends with a clear care recommendation. The customer walks out with something concrete, fast.

The price clears the friction. That doesn't mean cheap. There are two paths to a price that clears friction, and both work depending on the audience.

The first is a low absolute number. A $29 chef's tasting menu, a $99 HVAC tune-up, a $49 chiropractic assessment. Small dollar, small decision, the buyer can say yes without checking with anyone.

The second is a higher price paired with so much packed value that the cost-to-value ratio is obvious from the first sentence of the offer. The Weight-Loss Challenges I ran at JCF were the value-stacked kind, 6 to 8 weeks at $280 to $500. Every challenge included a nutrition program, group accountability, pre and post-program fitness evaluations, and the workouts JCF was known for. The price felt small relative to what someone was actually getting, and they converted at multiples of what a smaller-dollar entry point would have done.

Both paths work. The trap is pricing in the middle, a number too high to feel like a small decision and too low to signal premium value. That middle is where most FEOs go to die.

The cheap-and-fast path fits a high-volume, broad-reach audience, and the high-value-at-premium-price path fits a niche, high-intent audience that already understands what this kind of work normally costs. Pick the path that fits where your customer actually lives.

The other layer most owners miss is the upsell at the moment of transaction. Even when the FEO itself isn't profitable on the way in, the transaction is still a chance to build margin. A restaurant runs a $29 Couples Tasting and upsells a $35 wine-and-dessert package when the guests are seated. An HVAC company runs a $99 tune-up and has a written "while we're here" list of small fixes priced for same-day approval. A chiropractor runs a $49 assessment and offers a 3-pack of adjustments at the end of the appointment. The FEO clears the friction at the door, and the upsell at the moment of transaction is often where the margin lives.

It has a real deadline. "Book by Friday for our spring slot." "Limited to 20 new patients this month." "Available through May 30." The deadline is what turns "I should look into that someday" into "I need to call them this week." Most front-end offers fail because the door stays open forever and there's no reason to walk through it now.

It's a slice of the core, not a different thing. This is the part most owners get wrong. They build a front-end offer that doesn't naturally lead into anything else. A coupon for a free dessert at a restaurant doesn't lead anywhere except to a discount habit. A free coffee mug for HVAC customers doesn't lead anywhere except to a closet full of mugs. The front-end has to be a real preview of the core service so the natural next step is the core engagement itself.

Here's the math that usually convinces local owners to actually build one.

Run 100 cold leads at your core offer. Two convert if you're lucky, and even those two took multiple touches and a long sales cycle to get there. Run those same 100 cold leads at a well-designed front-end offer and 15 to 25 will convert. Of those 15 to 25 customers, somewhere between 40 and 60 percent move into the core offer over the following 90 days. Same lead pool, six to ten times the revenue, faster.

The mistake most owners make is trying to make money on the front-end itself. The front-end isn't supposed to be profitable. It's the cost of acquiring a customer who will be profitable over the next twelve months. Once you internalize that, the price stops being a problem and starts being a tool.

Here's how the same architecture shows up across different local businesses.

Restaurant. Front-end: a $59 chef's tasting menu for two, or a $45 wine pairing class on a slow Tuesday. Core: regular dinner traffic, private events, catering contracts, and birthday-club memberships.

HVAC. Front-end: a $99 seasonal system tune-up with a written report. Core: the $400 annual maintenance contract, plus the replacement systems that come with the relationship over time.

Home builder / custom cabinet shop. Front-end: a free in-home design consult with a written scope and a ballpark number. Core: the full build or installation.

Dentist. Front-end: a $99 new-patient exam plus cleaning plus whitening. Core: the ongoing patient relationship.

Real estate agent. Front-end: a free market analysis or buyer consultation with a written 90-day plan. Core: the listing or buyer engagement.

Chiropractor. Front-end: a $49 assessment plus first adjustment. Core: the multi-month treatment plan.

Same architecture, different package on the front. Every one of these owners can build a front-end version of their business in the next 30 days, and once it's running, the same marketing dollars convert at multiples of what they were.

Build the front-end first. Then we'll talk about how to drive traffic to it.

In My Stack

The Front-End Offer Designer I built for this issue. It's the fillable template I use with my coaching clients to walk through the seven decisions you need to make to design a front-end offer that converts strangers and feeds into your core offer.

The seven decisions, in order:

  1. Who — the exact audience the offer is built for (this is where the audit work from last week's issue pays off)
  2. Outcome — the specific result the customer walks away with
  3. Time window — how fast they get it
  4. Price — the friction-clearing number
  5. Deliverable — the slice of your core service that fits inside the front-end
  6. Deadline — the response date that creates urgency
  7. Bridge — the natural step that moves them into the core offer

Each decision has a fillable space, a worked example for six different verticals so you can see how it sounds when it's filled in, and a short note on the trap most owners walk into when they try to answer it on their own.

The Marketing Work Most People Skip

From the Floor

I spent a big chunk of Monday doing warm outreach on Facebook to grow the From The Floor Up subscriber list, and I picked it back up first thing this morning. Personal messages, one at a time, to people I'm connected with who I thought would actually use what I write about. Just a real note from me, a quick "this is what I'm publishing every Tuesday, here's the link, would love to have you on the list or get a share."

What I didn't expect was how much the replies were going to teach me.

As the confirmations started rolling back in, some from people who'd just joined and some from people letting me know they were already on the list, I started paying attention to who was actually opting in. There was a home builder I went to high school with. A guy running a diesel emissions testing shop. Restaurant owners. A real estate agent. A law firm partner. A physical therapist in a different state. A couple of fitness coaches. A handful of personal services owners I'd never have predicted would say yes.

When I sat down to write this morning, I wasn't sure what the commonality between them was. I spent more time than I usually do staring at the list, trying to find the connector between a home builder and an agent, between a restaurant operator and a law firm. That's also the reason this issue is hitting your inbox later in the day than I'd normally send it.

The thread became clear.

They all run local businesses, and most of them have been at it somewhere in the 3 to 10 plus year range. The idea took longer than I expected to land, and the issue took longer than my usual Tuesday turnaround to write. I wanted to make sure what came out of it was actually valuable for this mix of subscribers I'm just now starting to see clearly.

That's when I realized the next thing I had to make.

A series, thinking six parts (but that may shrink or grow), on how to market a local business. Tactical, vertical-agnostic, written so a Doer in year two or three can take action on it and an Owner CEO running multiple locations can sharpen their existing system from it.

It starts next Tuesday.

Before any of the marketing tactics will make sense though, I want to ground us in the move that has to come first. Because for most owners, the real marketing problem isn't where they keep looking.

The System

Marketing starts when you can describe your customer.

The trap most local owners walk into is spending money on the channel before defining the audience. A flyer drop in the wrong neighborhood, a billboard on a highway that aims at everyone and reaches no one, a workshop announced to a room of strangers who didn't ask for it. The channel isn't usually the problem in any of those cases. The audience is.

Defining who you're marketing to is the work that has to come first, and most local marketing money gets wasted because that work hasn't been done yet.

Here's the move I'd want every local business owner to make once a quarter.

Open your customer list, your client list, your booking history, whatever you've got that tells you who's already chosen you, and read the names. Not the numbers. The names. What was each of them trying to solve when they came to you. How did they find you. What kept them coming back, or what made them leave. The pattern that surfaces from that exercise is your audience, and your audience is the input every other marketing decision should run through.

The pattern you're looking for isn't a category. It's a person.

Pick one customer from the last twelve months who you'd love to have ten more of. Write down everything you actually know about them. Their age range, the neighborhood or area they live in, the stage of life they're in, the moment they reached out to you and what was going on in their world that day. The other places they probably looked before they found you, what they were nervous about before booking, and what they say about you now to friends and family. If you don't know this, be intentional with getting to know this customer by asking when they visit your establishment or by simply asking via email or phone.

If you can do that for one real customer with real specifics, you can probably do it for the ten or twenty customers behind them who came in for similar reasons. That cluster is your audience, and a real person you can describe in vivid detail gives you more to work with than any generic demographic profile.

Most local businesses serve at least two clusters. You might serve the busy professional and also the recent retiree, or the first-time homeowner and the third-time renovator, or the local family on a weeknight and the out-of-towner on a weekend. Knowing which cluster a given marketing message is for is half of what makes the message actually land with anyone.

The other piece worth naming is the maturity of the owner reading this, because that changes how you put any of this into practice. There are Doers running the work themselves with no real team, doing everything from delivery to invoicing in the same week. There are Managers running a small team of one to five people, still inside the marketing decisions every day. And there are Owner CEOs out of operations with marketing running through a team or an agency, often with multiple locations to think about. The series I'm about to run treats all three.

Get clear on who you're for. Then we'll talk about how to reach them.

In My Stack

The three-question customer interview I run on clients who've hired me. Takes about 10 minutes and surfaces more about who you actually serve than any survey, CRM report, or analytics tool will.

The questions, in order:

  1. How did you find me?
  2. What almost made you not book?*
  3. What's one thing you wish I knew about you that I don't?

The first tells you where to spend your marketing time, because it surfaces the channels that are already working without you noticing. The second pulls up the objections that are quietly costing you sales you'll never see in any report. The third gives you the language your next marketing message should sound like, in the customer's own words.

Run it on one customer/client this week. Take notes verbatim or record via voice memos app. Their answers become the input for your next marketing message.

The next six issues of From The Floor Up are a connected series, How to Market Your Local Business. Starts next Tuesday. Six parts, one a week, each with a free downloadable resource you can plug into your business. All six bundled into a single playbook at the end of the series for anyone who wants the whole thing in one place.

*For retail, this wording was part of our e-com brand post-purchase survey: What almost stopped you from completing your purchase?

Could you describe your ideal customer in enough vivid detail that someone who's never met them could spot them walking into your business tomorrow?

One Thing This Week

Pick the tier you're operating at right now and run the three-question interview on real customers this week.

Doer: Call one of your three favorite customers from the last six months. Ask the three questions. Record their answers. One call, 15 minutes, this week. One sentence from that call should reshape how you describe your business by Friday.

Manager: Run the interview on five of your top customers, and split the calls with someone on your team if it helps you cover more ground. Bring the verbatim answers to your next team meeting and find the patterns in the language. One marketing decision next week should come directly from a sentence one of those customers said.

Owner CEO: Block 30 minutes this week with whoever owns marketing for you. Pull the top 25 customers across locations. Run the interview on five of them yourself, have your team run it on the other twenty. Identify the two or three clearest clusters that emerge from the answers, and write a one-paragraph profile of each cluster in vivid detail. That paragraph becomes the brief for your next campaign.

The deeper your audit, the sharper everything that comes after it gets.

If you're recording the conversation, get permission beforehand.

The AI Workflow Behind Every Session I Run

From the Floor

A few weeks back I ran a coaching session with Mandi, one of my staging clients, on the floor of a vacant home she was staging in Central Park, Denver. It was the most unorthodox session I've ever held. She was directing the movers and her team, calling out furniture placement, and placing furniture. I was there to coach, jumping in to grab a piece of decor or carry something upstairs when it was the natural thing to do, but mostly trying to hold the thread of our conversation while the install unfolded around us. The session moved in fragments, with stretches of real talk between her and me, and then a pause while she stepped away to handle the next thing.

About 50 minutes in, while she was removing accessories from the containers and her team was assembling furniture, Mandi said something almost in passing. "I wish I could do that every day." She wasn't talking about the staging in front of her, she was talking about the week before. The week before, she didn't have to drive to the warehouse to load her truck. Her team handled it. She didn't have to be on site for the full install either, she showed up with about an hour left, did the styling touches she's best at, gave a few notes, and that was her contribution. Six weeks earlier, none of that would have happened. She would have been the warehouse picker and loader, the truck driver, the install lead, the stylist, and the closer all in the same body.

That sentence was the breakthrough of the whole engagement up to that point, and it was subtle, almost throwaway. In the middle of that chaos there's no version of the old me, with a notebook and a pen, that catches it. The voice memo running on my phone caught it. I uploaded the recording after I got back to my car, ran my Post-Session Capture workflow inside Cowork, and that exact line came back to me as a quote, attributed and ready to use. It went into the recap email I sent her the following morning and it sits in her profile in my Business Operating System in Notion as one of the most important shifts she's made.

The session itself was a proud coach moment for me. The capture was a proud system moment.

The System

Systems are never closed in a business, they're only iterated on, and AI collapsed the time of building them.

One thing I took away from running JCF, especially in the post-2013 years when I started building the systems with intention, is that the loop on documentation never really closes. We built the JCF Playbook as a living site, hosted on Google Sites, and on a good month we updated it once. In a tough season we were updating it every week. There was no moment, in fourteen years, where I thought the work of documenting how we operated was finished. Building the Playbook was an all-hands effort and a constant weight, and the reason it took years and not months was that the cost of capturing what we did, accurately and in the moment, was high.

That cost is what has changed for me with AI in the loop. The barrier to building and documenting a system used to be 90 to 95 percent of the work. Now it's 5 to 10 percent. The remaining piece is taste and judgment, what to keep, what to cut, what to teach the client back, and how to phrase it so it lands. The capture, the formatting, and the synthesis are all handled by the system.

Here's what closing the loop looks like for me after every coaching session today.

Before I close my laptop after the session ends, I name the voice memo. I copy and paste the transcript then I upload that with the Live Notes (more on this at a later time) I typed during the session into Cowork. The Live Notes are something I type because typing a few key things in real time helps me hold onto the details that matter to me and keeps me grounded in the conversation. Once those are in, I say "run my Post-Session Capture workflow," and the workflow produces two outputs. The first is a structured summary inside my Notion Business Operating System, formatted exactly the way I want it, with the session log entry, the verbatim quote, the moment that mattered most, and the open commitments for next session all in place. The second is a branded recap email sent to the client, with a recap of what we discussed, the client's commitment(s), the most important quote from the session highlighted in the body, and what's on the radar for the upcoming session. The whole thing takes me less than 30 minutes from session ended to loop closed.

Three C's of Hiring

From the Floor

Late 2017, Melissa S. left her admin role at JCF. My wife had filled in during past gaps in that seat, and with multiple young kids at home she couldn't cover this one.

It mattered to me to work with people I knew, liked, and trusted, and it mattered to the business that whoever held the seat understood the camaraderie that ran through every interaction. Yari, then Melissa S., then a long stretch of solid coverage in member relations and admin. Each of them carried JCF the way the brand deserved to be carried. The hiring system around them was real, with personality tests, multiple rounds of interviews, and a probationary period. We had collaborated to write the JCF Playbook in Google Sites, and the playbook covered most of what the role needed to do day to day. From the outside, the hiring machine looked dialed.

Inside, I was overwhelmed. The seat had been open too long, and I needed someone on the floor of the office handling the steady, unglamorous admin work yesterday. I ran the candidates through the process and it came down to three. The hire I made was someone I knew. Our kids went to school together. She was funny and warm and fit the JCF crowd. I liked her and trusted her, so I hired her.

She didn't last. The admin tasks were the main reason the seat existed, and the work was inconsistent and the call-outs piled up. Several months after her probationary period ended it was clear the role wasn't right for her, and that became the only fire I made at JCF.

Almost a decade later, what has stayed with me is that the failure was above her. I had built a real hiring process and walked it past two of my own rules to make a hire that fit the moment. I had hired in a state of overwhelm because the seat had been empty too long, and I had hired without being clear on what JCF the brand actually needed in that chair, which was the steady administrative consistency of running the same systems day after day. I had hired for the wrong job.

The System

Run the three Cs before you post the job.

Hiring is the most expensive decision in a service business and the most leveraged. When it goes wrong, it costs you more than money. It costs you the trust of the rest of the team, the time you sink into managing the wrong fit, and in some cases the seat itself, because once you've hired into it badly you've now changed the role for the next person.

The three things I check before I post a job:

Calm. Before anything else, I check my own state. If I'm hiring because the pain has to stop right now, I'm going to weight the decision toward speed and away from fit. I'll over-promise on comp, skip vetting steps I'd otherwise take seriously, and write a job description that is really a wishlist for the pain to go away. The hire shows up and the pain doesn't actually leave, because the wrong filter ran the decision.

Cleanup. Next comes the system the person is walking into. A new hire can't fix a system, they inherit it. If the work isn't documented, the SOPs don't exist, and the workflow runs on the founder's discretion, the new hire becomes another improviser inside the same mess. That feels slightly less lonely for a few weeks, and the chaos comes right back, this time with payroll attached.

Clarity. The last check is whether I can name the actual problem this role would solve, and the actual current solution holding things together right now. Most "I need to hire" moments are really "I need to redesign this process" or "I need to redistribute work that's already on the team." Until I can name the real problem and the duct tape currently holding it together, I don't know what role I'm trying to fill.

In late 2017 I had Cleanup. The Playbook existed, the personality tests were built, the onboarding was sequenced. What I didn't have was Calm or Clarity. I was hiring from overwhelm, and I was hiring for a role I hadn't accurately defined to myself. The hiring machinery was real, and it didn't save me. If those aren't right, the rest of the process can run perfectly and you'll still make the wrong hire.

In My Stack

I've built a Business Operating System inside Notion that houses my coaching systems, my course modules, my templates, and the SOPs for the work I run every week. The reason it works is that I almost never sit down to write SOPs from scratch. I do the work first, and after a session or a project that introduces a new task or workflow, I tell Cowork "analyze what we just completed and create an SOP." Cowork drafts the SOP in the format I've already taught it to use, I tweak the draft live in the session window, and once it's dialed in I say "add to my notion" and it uploads into the right database in the right structure.

That loop is how the Operating System grows. Every new piece of work becomes a documented system the next time I run it, and the documentation gets built in the moment instead of weeks later when I've forgotten the details.

The show rate wasn't telling me anything about my selling. It was telling me something about my calendar.

The System

Your broken number is probably a setup problem, not a skill problem.

When a number in your business is off, the first instinct is to practice the thing attached to that number. If sales are low, sharpen the pitch. If retention is low, rework the onboarding. If appointments run over time, go faster. That instinct makes sense, and sometimes the skill really is the issue. A skill problem lives inside the action, and you can fix it with practice and feedback. A setup problem lives one level above the action, in how your business is arranged, and practice won't fix it. You can run a perfect sales call on a calendar that books prospects five days out, and the show rate will still sit below 50 percent, because the setup is working against you.

The difference shows up clearly in the examples. A skill problem is inside the action itself, like a sales call that isn't closing or a coaching call that isn't landing. A setup problem sits one layer up, like leads coming in through people you don't control, a calendar that books prospects five days out, or a team that can't do the work without you in the room. You fix the first kind with practice and reps. You fix the second kind by changing the structure of the business.

For most service business owners the setup problem sitting under everything else is that the business still runs through you. Until that changes, every downstream number is carrying the weight of your calendar. Fixing my show rate meant building the coaching bench brick by brick so the studio could run without me on the floor for most of the sessions. The show rate was never a sales problem. It was a setup problem with my name on it.

Stop Teaching From Scratch, Start Recording

The real shift was realizing the experience didn't have to live entirely on the coach. When the environment is designed right, the members reinforce it too. The hi-fives snowball. The System

Stop teaching, start recording.

None of what I just described started as a manual. It started as me paying attention to what was already working and making sure it didn't disappear. The process documents came after the process existed, not before.

That shift matters more than it sounds. When you try to teach someone your process, you're forcing yourself to reconstruct it from memory. You're describing what you think you do, which is often different from what you actually do. The translation between what's in your head and what lands on paper creates gaps. Instructions get skipped because they seem obvious. Context gets lost. The person reading the manual still can't recreate the thing.

When you record the process instead, you skip the translation problem entirely. You're not asking yourself to describe the thing — you're capturing the thing as it's happening. The document comes from the execution, not from your best attempt to explain the execution.

I watched the same pattern play out this past Friday with a client. She'd been doing this for years, and she still stumbled a little at first, because she'd never had to put language to something that had become automatic.

That's what I call your Doer IP — the intellectual property of your craft that lives in your instincts rather than any document. Every skilled service provider has it. Most of them have never extracted it.

Once she got rolling, I had her explain it again but this time record a video and narrate it as if she was training a new hire. Then we had the transcript cleaned up and we had the beginning of her full process, her Styling IP, including her standard for styling dining tables and the specific things she considers when making those decisions. None of it was new to her. She'd been doing it for years. But she'd never seen it as a transferable system because nobody had ever asked the right questions to pull it out of her.

Downloading your Doer IP requires a different frame. You can't sit at a desk and write it from memory. You have to be in the work, doing the thing, and have someone pressing you on why you're making the choices you're making. The document comes from the motion, not the other way around.

Your Doer IP is already running. Every time you deliver your service on a good day, the system is right there. You just need someone to press record.

The System

For a long time, the question I kept asking myself was whether anyone on my team could do the work as well as I could. The answer was usually no, or not yet, or not in exactly the same way. So I kept doing it myself, and the business kept depending on me doing it, and I kept adding more hours until the hours were all there was.

I had been hustling and grinding with the best of them and still had a business that could not work without me.

Watching that was uncomfortable in a productive way. It gave me a target. More importantly, it made me question something I'd never really examined.

I stopped asking if anyone else could do it as well as me, and I started asking a different question: if this absolutely had to happen without me, what would it take?

Better questions give you better answers. I truly believe this. That question allowed me to move out of comparison and into design. It made me think about what someone would actually need to know, what they'd need access to, what the steps are when they're written down instead of living only in your head. It turns a vague dependency into a documented process.

It's also THE question behind every system worth building in a service business. Not who else could do this, but what would it take. Discretion Is the Absence of Systems

From the Floor

When I ran JCF Boot Camp in Jersey City, I had a team of coaches. Good ones. They cared about the members, they showed up on time, and they brought real energy to every session.

But every single one of them ran sessions differently. One opened the huddle with a pep talk. Another skipped it and went straight into the demo. One did a full breakdown at the end with detailed coaching notes, another wrapped with a quick high-five and sent everyone home. The members noticed. Some loved Tuesdays and dreaded Thursdays, not because of the workout but because the experience felt like a different gym depending on who was leading.

I was frustrated at first. I thought I had a coaching problem. Then I realized I had a systems problem. I had never written down what a JCF session actually looked like from start to finish.

So I built what I called the Anatomy of a JCF Session. One page. It defined the structure every coach followed:

Members warm up on their own before the session starts. Huddle at the top to set the intention and prime the session, ending with the "Better Body. Better Life." breakdown. Demo. Pre-workout. Main workout. Finisher. Cool down with announcements. Post breakdown: "All day. Everyday."

That was it. No script for what to say during the huddle, no requirement that every coach sound like me. The structure was the standard. The personality on top of it was theirs.

The moment that document existed, the member experience became recognizable no matter who was leading. Tuesdays felt like JCF. Thursdays felt like JCF. Because it wasn't the Daris way anymore. It was the JCF way.

The System

Discretion is the absence of systems.

Every place in your business where a team member is using their own judgment to fill a gap is a place where you haven't built a system yet. That's not a criticism of the person. Their judgment is often reasonable, sometimes good. But it's inconsistent by definition, because judgment varies from person to person and from day to day, and the client experience shifts with it.

My coaches weren't doing anything wrong. They were doing what every capable person does when the instructions are vague: they improvised. And improvisation across a team produces variety, not consistency.

This is the part most service business owners resist, because it feels like building a standard removes the personal touch. It doesn't. It protects it. When the structure is consistent, the personality on top of it has room to breathe. When the structure is missing, the personality is doing all the work, and the experience depends entirely on which team member shows up that day.

The Anatomy of a JCF Session didn't tell coaches what to say or how to coach. It told them what the session looks like, the order of events, the bookends, the non-negotiable touchpoints. Within that structure, every coach brought their own style and their own energy, and the members got the benefit of both: a consistent experience and a human one.

The question worth sitting with is not "is my team doing it wrong." The question is "have I defined what the experience should feel like in a way that doesn't depend on me being in the room."

If the answer is no, your team is operating on discretion. And discretion, across a team, produces exactly what you're seeing: inconsistency that feels like a people problem but is actually an infrastructure problem.

The standard doesn't belong to you. It belongs to the company. When you build it that way, the company can hold it whether you're there or not.

In My Stack

I've been using Notion as the home base for all of the system documents in my coaching practice. One workspace, one template, every system linked to the others. The thing I like about it is that the documents are alive. I can update a process in two minutes and everyone with access sees the current version immediately. No emailing updated PDFs, no "which version is the latest" conversations. If you're still storing your operating documents in scattered Google Docs or text threads, try consolidating them into one workspace. The clarity of having everything in one place is worth the afternoon it takes to set up.

One Thing This Week

Write down three things: what the experience should look like from start to finish, which moments are non-negotiable, and what the client should walk away feeling. That's not a full system document. That's the anatomy of your version of a session, and it's the foundation the document gets built from.
