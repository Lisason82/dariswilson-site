The Story

16 years of building from the inside.

My name is Daris, like Paris but with a D. I've been building businesses since 2008 across fitness, e-commerce, and coaching, and every lesson I teach comes from something I built with my own money on the line. I went from All-Conference D1 athlete to accountant to the regulation side of the NYSE to full-time entrepreneur, and I've spent the better part of two decades figuring out what it takes to build a business that actually works when the owner steps out of the room.

2005
Bucknell University, BSBA in Accounting. Born and raised in New Orleans, graduated after four years as an All-Conference Division I athlete. Hurricane Katrina changed the trajectory and brought me to New York, where I started my career on the regulation side of the New York Stock Exchange.
2008
Jersey City Fitness (JCF). Built the first boutique fitness business in Jersey City, NJ while still working full-time at the NYSE. Early mornings before the opening bell, late nights after market close, and guerrilla marketing in a city that didn't have anything like it yet.
2010
Full-time entrepreneur. Left the NYSE on March 9, 2010. I had clients, but I sat down at a Starbucks on Grove Street the next morning with no safety net and started building the business around them. That morning is the reason this newsletter launches on March 10.
2013
The reset. Three years into running the business full-time, the model broke. Revenue was inconsistent, the business ran entirely through me, and I had no real systems to speak of. I hired a business coach, invested in learning how to sell and how to lead, and that decision reshaped every business I've built since.
2020
COVID pivot. Moved JCF online when the world shut down and kept the community together through a year that closed most fitness businesses permanently. Ran JCF for 14 years total, 2008 through 2022.
2021
Terrell Dominick LLC. Co-founded an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife. $40K to $85K in monthly revenue, with me running operations, marketing, and manufacturing. A completely different industry built on the same systems thinking.
2026
From The Floor Up + The Staged CEO™. Launched a weekly newsletter for service business owners and started building The Staged CEO Accelerator, a 12-week program for home staging entrepreneurs who are ready to stop doing the work and start running the company. The work continues.

How the Accent Wall Became a Moat

From the Floor

We called it the 12-Week Rock My Skinny Jeans Challenge. Day one at JCF Boot Camp was intake day, which meant body assessments, measurements, and progress photos in front of the accent wall at the coworking space where we ran our orientations. One space had a green wall and another had a coral-ish one, and we ended up shooting the before and after photos in the same rooms over and over, which meant every finished transformation ran the same visual signature across the entire library. We didn't plan that at the start, it was accidental, and it became one of the few things competitors couldn't copy. Day forty-two was closeout day, which meant the matching set of photos, a survey, and a short video testimonial. By the time a participant finished the program, we already had everything a prospect would need to see to believe it was real.

The System

Make proof part of the program.

Before the Skinny Jeans Challenge, I wasn't really asking clients for reviews. I'd mention it in a group setting occasionally, hope someone heard me, and move on. My social proof was thin, and the reason wasn't that my clients weren't happy. It was that I'd built a business where proof was optional and depended on a client choosing to go out of their way to produce it.

What changed in 2013 wasn't that I got better at asking. It was that I stopped asking and started building. The before photos happened because the program started with a body assessment. The after photos happened because the program ended with one. The video testimonials and survey feedback happened because the final session was structured as a wrap-up with a camera already set up. A participant who completed the twelve weeks couldn't help but generate proof, because proof was a scheduled step inside the service, not a favor we asked for at the end.

Most service business owners have the relationship with their clients they'd need for incredible social proof. What they don't have is a delivery process that produces that proof automatically. If the only way to get a review, a testimonial, a case study, or a referral from a client is to remember to ask after the job is done, you're running on a system that depends on your mood and your memory, and that system will fail you on the weeks you need it most.

The shift is to treat proof as a deliverable inside the service rather than an ask at the end. That could look like intake questions that capture what the client hoped to get from working with you, mid-engagement touchpoints that capture what's already working, and closeout sessions that capture the outcome in the client's own words while the experience is still fresh. When the service is designed to produce proof, you don't have to chase it later, it shows up on the calendar the same way the actual work does.

In My Stack

This week I'm sending subscribers the Social Proof Playbook. Inside is the intake questionnaire I'd use to engineer proof from day one of a client engagement, a closeout question set for capturing outcomes in the client's own voice, and a 7 to 14 day follow-up template for turning those captured answers into public reviews in the places that matter most for your business. Reply to this email with the word PROOF and I'll send it over.

If I made no extra asks of my clients this year and only collected the proof my current service process naturally produces, what would I actually have at the end of the twelve months.

One Thing This Week

Pick one step in your service delivery, either intake, midpoint, or closeout, and add one scheduled moment inside that step where the client's answer, quote, or result gets captured as part of the process rather than as a favor you ask for afterward.

800 Leads in One Day

From the Floor

In 2015, I set up a table at the All About Downtown Street Fair in Jersey City. It's the biggest street fair in the city, tens of thousands of residents walking through in a single day, and for a local fitness business like JCF Boot Camp, it felt like the perfect opportunity. So I went out there and tried to sell memberships on the spot. I had conversations, handed out flyers, gave the pitch. By the end of the day I had less than a handful of signups, sore feet, and the feeling that I'd left a massive opportunity on the table.

I spent the next year thinking about what went wrong. The reach was there. Ten to twenty thousand people walking past my table in a day. The problem wasn't the audience. The problem was that I was trying to close strangers on a $150/month commitment in a 90-second conversation while they were eating funnel cake and chasing their kids. The strategy didn't match the moment.

The System

Build your spin wheel.

When I came back in 2016, everything was different. I invested in a full branded canopy kit with walls and flags, a branded tablecloth, a monitor running testimonial videos and class footage as social proof, an iPad to capture contact information, and a big colorful spin wheel right at the front of the table. To spin the wheel you had to leave your name, email, and phone number on the iPad. You could win JCF swag, a branded cell phone stand, a JCF tote bag, or a free week of classes. The system was what mattered.

Our tent was buzzing the entire day. People saw the spin wheel from 30 feet away, walked over with their families, entered their info, spun, grabbed their prize, and left smiling. By the end of the day we had collected over 800 local leads. Not followers, not impressions, not people who maybe saw a flyer. Eight hundred names, emails, and phone numbers from people who lived in our city and had just had a positive interaction with our brand.

The principle underneath that story is one I want every service business owner to understand: you don't need to build reach from scratch. You need to borrow it from platforms and events that already have the audience you want, and then create a system that siphons those people into your world where you can nurture the relationship on your terms.

All About Downtown had 10,000 to 20,000 people walking through those streets. For me to get that many local residents to walk past JCF doors would have taken years of marketing and thousands of dollars. But the street fair had already done that work. My job was to build a net that caught the right people out of the crowd and brought them into my universe.

Here's how the framework breaks down, and it applies to far more than street fairs.

1. Identify a platform with more reach than you. This could be a local event, a social media platform, a booking app like StyleSeat or Booksy, a local Facebook group, a community organization, or a partnership with a complementary business. The question is simple: who already has the attention of the people I want to serve.

2. Design a lead capture mechanism. Something that gives the person a reason to hand you their contact information in exchange for something small and immediate. At the street fair it was the spin wheel. Online it might be a free guide, a checklist, a quiz, a giveaway, a discount code, or a booking link that collects their info before confirming. The mechanism has to match the moment.

3. Execute with intention. We didn't just show up with a folding table and a jar of business cards. We debriefed the previous year, got clear on the goal (leads, not sales), strategized the entire setup, and invested in the experience at the booth. The branded canopy, the monitor, the spin wheel, the prizes. Every element had a job. Nothing was accidental.

4. Nurture the leads you captured. This is where most people drop the ball. You collect 800 leads and then send one email a week later and wonder why nobody signed up. The leads don't know you yet. They had a 45-second interaction at a street fair. What they gave you was permission to start a conversation, not a commitment. We followed up within 48 hours, offered a low-barrier trial, and built the relationship from there.

This same framework works digitally right now. If you're a service business owner posting on Instagram, your content is the spin wheel. Your bio link is the iPad. Your email list or booking system is the net. Instagram has the crowd. Your job is to get the people who resonate with your content off the platform and into a channel you own — your email list, your text list, your CRM — somewhere the algorithm can't decide whether they see you next week.

The street fair taught me something I've carried into every business since. You don't grow by trying to build a crowd. You grow by showing up where the crowd already is and building a system that brings the right people home with you.

In My Stack

This week I'm sharing a lead capture planner I built for my own use and I'm making it available to every subscriber. It's a one-page framework that walks you through the four steps from today's system: identify your platform, design your capture mechanism, plan your execution, and map your nurture sequence. I use a version of this every time I launch a new lead gen campaign, whether it's an event, a social media push, or a partnership. Reply to this email with "LEADS" and I'll send it to you.

The leads that came into my business the last 30–60 days — what percentage of them came from sources I own (email, text list, CRM) vs. sources I do not own (social media, booking app)? The goal is to get those leads onto your owned list as quickly as you can.

One Thing This Week

Pick one platform — whether it's Instagram, TikTok, a local Facebook group, a booking app like Booksy or StyleSeat, or an upcoming community event — and build one specific mechanism that captures contact information from that platform into a list you own.

Stop Teaching From Scratch, Start Recording

From the Floor

When I started developing coaches at JCF Boot Camp, I didn't know where to begin. I'd hand off the workout the night before, do a quick Q&A, and send them out there. The sessions were fine. But fine wasn't the standard. One of our tenets was "be the best part of our Members' day," and you can't deliver on that with average energy and a printed workout.

The hard part was figuring out what was transferable and what wasn't. I didn't want clones of me. My energy, my Daris'isms, the way I naturally worked a room at 5:30 in the morning, that was mine. But the experience members came back for wasn't just my personality. It was the feeling in the space, the camaraderie, the sense that you walked into a team, not a gym class. I'd been part of teams since I was five years old, and building that environment came naturally to me. But natural for me didn't mean obvious for everyone else.

So I started breaking down the pieces. I looked at what members told us they loved, through surveys, unsolicited feedback, things people said on the way out the door, and I separated what was uniquely me from what could be designed into the experience itself. Learning every member's name, no compromise. Making sure every person in the room got a hi-five and at least one form touch, either positive reinforcement or a correction, from a coach during the session. Call and response chants that built energy even when a coach was quieter by nature. Introducing yourself to every new face. These weren't personality traits. These were systems, and they created the energy in the room whether I was leading or not.

The real shift was realizing the experience didn't have to live entirely on the coach. When the environment is designed right, the members reinforce it too. The hi-fives snowball. The chants carry themselves. A new person walks in and the culture tells them how this place works before a coach ever says a word. I built JCF Coach University for new hires and ongoing team development. I shadowed coaches and they shadowed me. I ran anonymous coaching surveys so members could give honest feedback. And over time, the picture got clearer. My coaching IP could be broken into parts, some went into coaching systems and development, and some went into the member experience so it wasn't all resting on one person's shoulders. We all took responsibility in shaping the JCF experience.

The System

Stop teaching, start recording.

None of what I just described started as a manual. It started as me paying attention to what was already working and making sure it didn't disappear. The process documents came after the process existed, not before.

That shift matters more than it sounds. When you try to teach someone your process, you're forcing yourself to reconstruct it from memory. You're describing what you think you do, which is often different from what you actually do. The translation between what's in your head and what lands on paper creates gaps. Instructions get skipped because they seem obvious. Context gets lost. The person reading the manual still can't recreate the thing.

When you record the process instead, you skip the translation problem entirely. You're not asking yourself to describe the thing — you're capturing the thing as it's happening. The document comes from the execution, not from your best attempt to explain the execution.

I watched the same pattern play out this past Friday with a client. She was on-site at a staging and I asked her to walk me through what she was doing as she did it — not explain it to me, just narrate her choices out loud. Why she was pulling this piece into the space. What she was looking for when she decided the table needed something else. How she knew when a room was done. She'd been doing this for years, and she still stumbled a little at first, because she'd never had to put language to something that had become automatic.

That's what I call your Doer IP — the intellectual property of your craft that lives in your instincts rather than any document. Every skilled service provider has it. Most of them have never extracted it.

Once she got rolling, I had her explain it again but this time record a video and narrate it as if she was training a new hire. Then we had the transcript cleaned up and we had the beginning of her full process, her Styling IP, including her standard for styling dining tables and the specific things she considers when making those decisions. None of it was new to her. She'd been doing it for years. But she'd never seen it as a transferable system because nobody had ever asked the right questions to pull it out of her.

Downloading your Doer IP requires a different frame. You can't sit at a desk and write it from memory. You have to be in the work, doing the thing, and have someone pressing you on why you're making the choices you're making. The document comes from the motion, not the other way around.

Your Doer IP is already running. Every time you deliver your service on a good day, the system is right there. You just need someone to press record.

In My Stack

I've been using voice memos as a process capture tool. After a coaching session or a workflow that went well, I'll open the voice memo app and narrate what just happened in real time while it's still fresh. What I did, in what order, and why it worked. Then I use an AI tool to clean up the transcript and pull the steps into a document. It takes five minutes and produces a better process doc than I'd get sitting down for an hour trying to write one from memory. If you've been putting off building your SOPs because the blank page feels overwhelming, try talking through what you just did instead of trying to write what you should do.

If someone followed you through your best day, start to finish, and wrote down every step you took without you explaining any of it, what would that document look like?

One Thing This Week

The next time you're doing a task in the business — creating a proposal, building a workout program, writing descriptions, etc. — record your screen or yourself doing the thing and narrate what you're doing in a way a new hire could understand and execute the task. If you conclude someone with no connection to your business can complete the task at 85% or higher satisfaction, your system is complete.

16 Years Ago Today

From the Floor

March 10, 2010, I was sitting at a Starbucks on Grove Street in downtown Jersey City with a laptop, a fitness business I'd been building on the side for two years, and no paycheck coming on Friday. The day before, I'd walked out of the NYSE for the last time. That morning, I had to figure out what came next.

It was apparent I had become indispensable to the business I owned and I had finally understood that being indispensable was the problem, not the sign of success.

I had built a fitness business in that city while working full time at the exchange. Two years of showing up before and after market hours, being the trainer, the scheduler, the problem-solver, and the person every client needed to speak with personally. The business existed because I showed up. The moment I didn't, it paused.

That was the thing I needed to fix, the way the business was built.

Sixteen years later, I am launching this newsletter on the same date I sat down at that table. The work has changed. The lesson hasn't.

This is From The Floor Up. Every Tuesday, one real lesson for service business owners who are building something and starting to feel the weight of having built it around themselves.

The System

Here is how it starts. You are good at what you do. Clients notice. Referrals come in. You bring on some help, but the work always runs better when you are there. So you stay involved more than you planned.

At some point, "I'll just do it" becomes your operating model. Not because you are a control freak. Because you are fast, you care, and your standards are high. It makes complete sense in the moment. The problem is what it builds over time: a business that cannot function without you in the room.

The diagnostic is simple. If you took two full weeks away from your business, phone off and email unread, what would actually happen?

Most service business owners know the answer before they finish asking the question. The business slows. Things fall through. Clients get anxious. Team members wait.

That answer is not a judgment. It is a starting point.

The work of building a business that can run without you is harder than being excellent at what you do. Most owners never start it. This newsletter is for the ones who are ready to.

In My Stack

I've been using Claude Cowork to build out the operating routines for this newsletter and it's helped me stay on track. Instead of starting each session wondering what to work on, I have a morning routine that tells me exactly where I am and what needs to happen. I check things off. I move forward.

It's easy and that's the point.

If you're a service business owner who's constantly rebuilding your workflow from scratch at the start of every week, this is worth 20 minutes of your time. Set up one routine, just your Monday morning check-in, and see if it keeps you on track.

What would happen to my business if I took off the next two weeks? What would have to be in place to avoid those things from happening?

The checklist should have: a) Objective b) the steps to complete the task (checklist) c) screenshots or links to screen recordings d) "What success looks like" section so the one completing the task can own it and have zero discretion on completing the task effectively.

Don't overcomplicate it. Simply, list the steps.

You've Been Asking the Wrong Question

From the Floor

For years, I believed the clients at JCF were there because of me, because of the way I coached and the energy I brought to that floor at 5am. Maybe some of that was true. But that belief had a cost I couldn't see yet, and it took working 90+ hour weeks and missing nights at home with my kids before I had to honestly reckon with what I'd actually built.

The System

For a long time, the question I kept asking myself was whether anyone on my team could do the work as well as I could. The answer was usually no, or not yet, or not in exactly the same way. So I kept doing it myself, and the business kept depending on me doing it, and I kept adding more hours until the hours were all there was.

I had been hustling and grinding with the best of them and still had a business that could not work without me.

The shift came slowly, and it came from being in rooms with other fitness business owners who were running the same kind of operation I was, but differently, with more ease, more capacity, and actual lives outside the business. Watching that was uncomfortable in a productive way. It gave me a target. More importantly, it made me question something I'd never really examined.

I stopped asking if anyone else could do it as well as me, and I started asking a different question: if this absolutely had to happen without me, what would it take?

Better questions give you better answers. I truly believe this. That question allowed me to move out of comparison and into design. It made me think about what someone would actually need to know, what they'd need access to, what the steps are when they're written down instead of living only in your head. It turns a vague dependency into a documented process.

It's also THE question behind every system worth building in a service business. Not who else could do this, but what would it take. When you start there, you stop being the answer to every problem and start being the person who builds the answer instead, and that's a much better place to run a business from.

In My Stack

If you want a head start, copy this prompt into Claude or ChatGPT and let it walk you through the whole thing. It will ask you questions, you answer them, and it builds the document for you.

You are a business systems coach helping a service-based business owner document one repeatable task so someone else could do it without asking a single question. Ask me to describe the task I want to get off my plate. Then help me build a simple system document with these four sections: 1. What this task is and why it matters to the business 2. What someone would need to know before starting 3. What they would need access to (tools, logins, files, contacts) 4. The steps, written in plain language, in order Keep the language simple. Do not use corporate jargon. Write as if you are explaining this to a smart, capable person who has never done this specific task before. Ask me one question at a time and build the document as we go. When we are done, give me the full document formatted and ready to copy.

What task do you keep doing yourself because you think only you can do it right, and have you ever written down what "doing it right" actually means?

One Thing This Week

Pick one task you do every week and write down what someone would need to know, what they'd need to have access to, and the steps written down for someone else to do it without asking you a single question.

Hit reply and let me know the first task you're getting off your plate.

Discretion Is the Absence of Systems

From the Floor

When I ran JCF Boot Camp in Jersey City, I had a team of coaches. Good ones. They cared about the members, they showed up on time, and they brought real energy to every session.

But every single one of them ran sessions differently. One opened the huddle with a pep talk. Another skipped it and went straight into the demo. One did a full breakdown at the end with detailed coaching notes, another wrapped with a quick high-five and sent everyone home. The members noticed. Some loved Tuesdays and dreaded Thursdays, not because of the workout but because the experience felt like a different gym depending on who was leading.

I was frustrated at first. I thought I had a coaching problem. Then I realized I had a systems problem. I had never written down what a JCF session actually looked like from start to finish.

So I built what I called the Anatomy of a JCF Session. One page. It defined the structure every coach followed:

Members warm up on their own before the session starts. Huddle at the top to set the intention and prime the session, ending with the "Better Body. Better Life." breakdown. Demo. Pre-workout. Main workout. Finisher. Cool down with announcements. Post breakdown: "All day. Everyday."

That was it. No script for what to say during the huddle, no requirement that every coach sound like me. The structure was the standard. The personality on top of it was theirs.

The moment that document existed, the member experience became recognizable no matter who was leading. Tuesdays felt like JCF. Thursdays felt like JCF. Because it wasn't the Daris way anymore. It was the JCF way.

The System

Discretion is the absence of systems.

Every place in your business where a team member is using their own judgment to fill a gap is a place where you haven't built a system yet. That's not a criticism of the person. Their judgment is often reasonable, sometimes good. But it's inconsistent by definition, because judgment varies from person to person and from day to day, and the client experience shifts with it.

My coaches weren't doing anything wrong. They were doing what every capable person does when the instructions are vague: they improvised. And improvisation across a team produces variety, not consistency.

This is the part most service business owners resist, because it feels like building a standard removes the personal touch. It doesn't. It protects it. When the structure is consistent, the personality on top of it has room to breathe. When the structure is missing, the personality is doing all the work, and the experience depends entirely on which team member shows up that day.

The Anatomy of a JCF Session didn't tell coaches what to say or how to coach. It told them what the session looks like, the order of events, the bookends, the non-negotiable touchpoints. Within that structure, every coach brought their own style and their own energy, and the members got the benefit of both: a consistent experience and a human one.

The question worth sitting with is not "is my team doing it wrong." The question is "have I defined what the experience should feel like in a way that doesn't depend on me being in the room."

If the answer is no, your team is operating on discretion. And discretion, across a team, produces exactly what you're seeing: inconsistency that feels like a people problem but is actually an infrastructure problem.

The standard doesn't belong to you. It belongs to the company. When you build it that way, the company can hold it whether you're there or not.

In My Stack

I've been using Notion as the home base for all of the system documents in my coaching practice. One workspace, one template, every system linked to the others. The thing I like about it is that the documents are alive. I can update a process in two minutes and everyone with access sees the current version immediately. No emailing updated PDFs, no "which version is the latest" conversations.
