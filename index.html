The Story

16 years of building from the inside.

My name is Daris, like Paris but with a D. I've been building businesses since 2008 across fitness, e-commerce, and coaching, and every lesson I teach comes from something I built with my own money on the line. I went from All-Conference D1 athlete to accountant to the regulation side of the NYSE to full-time entrepreneur, and I've spent the better part of two decades figuring out what it takes to build a business that actually works when the owner steps out of the room.

2005
Bucknell University, BSBA in Accounting. Born and raised in New Orleans, graduated after four years as an All-Conference Division I athlete. Hurricane Katrina changed the trajectory and brought me to New York, where I started my career on the regulation side of the New York Stock Exchange.
2008
Jersey City Fitness (JCF). Built the first boutique fitness business in Jersey City, NJ while still working full-time at the NYSE. Early mornings before the opening bell, late nights after market close, and guerrilla marketing in a city that didn't have anything like it yet.
2010
Full-time entrepreneur. Left the NYSE on March 9, 2010. I had clients, but I sat down at a Starbucks on Grove Street the next morning with no safety net and started building the business around them. That morning is the reason this newsletter launches on March 10.
2013
The reset. Three years into running the business full-time, the model broke. Revenue was inconsistent, the business ran entirely through me, and I had no real systems to speak of. I hired a business coach, invested in learning how to sell and how to lead, and that decision reshaped every business I've built since.
2020
COVID pivot. Moved JCF online when the world shut down and kept the community together through a year that closed most fitness businesses permanently. Ran JCF for 14 years total, 2008 through 2022.
2021
Terrell Dominick LLC. Co-founded an e-commerce fashion brand with my wife. $40K to $85K in monthly revenue, with me running operations, marketing, and manufacturing. A completely different industry built on the same systems thinking.
2026
From The Floor Up + The Staged CEO™. Launched a weekly newsletter for service business owners and started building The Staged CEO Accelerator, a 12-week program for home staging entrepreneurs who are ready to stop doing the work and start running the company. The work continues.

Issue 004 · March 31, 2026 · 3 min read

The Standard Belongs to the Company

From the Floor

When I ran JCF Boot Camp in Jersey City, I had a team of trainers. Good ones. They cared about the clients, they showed up on time, and they brought real energy to every class.

But every single one of them ran the morning huddle differently. One opened with a pep talk. Another jumped straight into the warmup. One did a breakdown at the end with detailed coaching notes, another wrapped with a quick high-five and sent everyone home. The clients noticed. Some loved Tuesdays and dreaded Thursdays, not because of the workout but because the experience felt like a different gym depending on who was leading.

I was frustrated at first. I thought I had a coaching problem. Then I realized I had a systems problem. I had never written down what a JCF morning huddle actually looked like. I had never defined what the breakdown should cover or how long it should take. Every trainer was doing their version of what they thought I wanted, and since I had never documented what I wanted, their discretion became the operating standard.

That was the moment I stopped trying to get my team to do it "the Daris way" and started building the JCF way.

The System

Discretion is the absence of systems.

Every place in your business where a team member is using their own judgment to fill a gap is a place where you haven't built a system yet. That's not a criticism of the team member. Their judgment is often reasonable, sometimes even good. But it's inconsistent by definition, because judgment varies from person to person and from day to day, and the client experience shifts with it.

The JCF trainers weren't doing anything wrong. They were doing what every capable person does when the instructions are vague: they improvised. And improvisation produces variety, not consistency.

The fix was not telling each trainer to do it my way. That approach has a ceiling, because "my way" only works when I'm there to demonstrate it or correct the deviation. The fix was defining the company way and putting it on paper.

For us that meant writing down three things: what the morning huddle covers (welcome, the plan for the day, one coaching cue to focus on), how long the breakdown takes (3 minutes, not 10), and what the client should walk away feeling (seen, challenged, and clear on what they did well). Those three standards didn't script the trainers. They still brought their personality and their coaching style. But the structure held, and the experience became recognizable no matter who was leading.

This is the shift most service business owners resist because it feels like it removes the personal touch. It doesn't. It protects it. When the structure is consistent, the personality on top of it has room to breathe. When the structure is missing, the personality is doing all the work, and the client experience depends entirely on which team member shows up that day.

The question to ask yourself is not "is my team doing it wrong." The question is "have I defined what right looks like in a way that doesn't depend on me being in the room."

If the answer is no, every team member is operating on discretion. And discretion, across a team, produces exactly what you're seeing: inconsistency that feels like a people problem but is actually an infrastructure problem.

The standard doesn't belong to you. It belongs to the company. When you build it that way, the company can hold the standard whether you're there or not.

In My Stack

I've been using Notion as the home base for all of the system documents I build for my coaching practice. One database, one template, every system linked to the others. The thing I like about it is that the documents are alive. I can update a process in two minutes and everyone with access sees the current version. No emailing updated PDFs, no "which version is the latest" conversations. If you're still storing your SOPs in Google Docs scattered across folders, try consolidating them into one Notion workspace. The clarity of having everything in one place is worth the afternoon it takes to set up.

If you pulled three different team members aside today and asked each one to describe how they handle the same task in your business, would you get three versions of the same answer or three completely different answers.

One Thing This Week

Pick one task your team does where you've noticed the result varies depending on who does it. Write down three things: what it should look like when it's done, how long it should take, and what the client should experience. That's not a full SOP. That's the standard the SOP gets built from.

16 Years Ago Today

From the Floor

March 10, 2010, I was sitting at a Starbucks on Grove Street in downtown Jersey City with a laptop, a fitness business I'd been building on the side for two years, and no paycheck coming on Friday. The day before, I'd walked out of the NYSE for the last time. That morning, I had to figure out what came next.

It was apparent I had become indispensable to the business I owned and I had finally understood that being indispensable was the problem, not the sign of success.

I had built a fitness business in that city while working full time at the exchange. Two years of showing up before and after market hours, being the trainer, the scheduler, the problem-solver, and the person every client needed to speak with personally. The business existed because I showed up. The moment I didn't, it paused.

That was the thing I needed to fix, the way the business was built.

Sixteen years later, I am launching this newsletter on the same date I sat down at that table. The work has changed. The lesson hasn't.

This is From The Floor Up. Every Tuesday, one real lesson for service business owners who are building something and starting to feel the weight of having built it around themselves.

The System

Here is how it starts. You are good at what you do. Clients notice. Referrals come in. You bring on some help, but the work always runs better when you are there. So you stay involved more than you planned.

At some point, "I'll just do it" becomes your operating model. Not because you are a control freak. Because you are fast, you care, and your standards are high. It makes complete sense in the moment. The problem is what it builds over time: a business that cannot function without you in the room.

The diagnostic is simple. If you took two full weeks away from your business, phone off and email unread, what would actually happen?

Most service business owners know the answer before they finish asking the question. The business slows. Things fall through. Clients get anxious. Team members wait.

That answer is not a judgment. It is a starting point.

The work of building a business that can run without you is harder than being excellent at what you do. Most owners never start it. This newsletter is for the ones who are ready to.

In My Stack

I've been using Claude Cowork to build out the operating routines for this newsletter and it's helped me stay on track. Instead of starting each session wondering what to work on, I have a morning routine that tells me exactly where I am and what needs to happen. I check things off. I move forward.

It's easy and that's the point.

If you're a service business owner who's constantly rebuilding your workflow from scratch at the start of every week, this is worth 20 minutes of your time. Set up one routine, just your Monday morning check-in, and see if it keeps you on track.

What would happen to my business if I took off the next two weeks? What would have to be in place to avoid those things from happening?

One Thing This Week

Based on your answer(s) to "Ask Yourself", prioritize the top 3 and complete a checklist for each. The checklist should have: a) Objective b) the steps to complete the task (checklist) c) screenshots or links to screen recordings d) "What success looks like" section so the one completing the task can own it and have zero discretion on completing the task effectively.

Don't overcomplicate it. Simply, list the steps.

You've Been Asking the Wrong Question

From the Floor

For years, I believed the clients at JCF were there because of me, because of the way I coached and the energy I brought to that floor at 5am. Maybe some of that was true. But that belief had a cost I couldn't see yet, and it took working 90+ hour weeks and missing nights at home with my kids before I had to honestly reckon with what I'd actually built.

The System

For a long time, the question I kept asking myself was whether anyone on my team could do the work as well as I could. The answer was usually no, or not yet, or not in exactly the same way. So I kept doing it myself, and the business kept depending on me doing it, and I kept adding more hours until the hours were all there was.

I had been hustling and grinding with the best of them and still had a business that could not work without me.

The shift came slowly, and it came from being in rooms with other fitness business owners who were running the same kind of operation I was, but differently, with more ease, more capacity, and actual lives outside the business. Watching that was uncomfortable in a productive way. It gave me a target. More importantly, it made me question something I'd never really examined.

I stopped asking if anyone else could do it as well as me, and I started asking a different question: if this absolutely had to happen without me, what would it take?

Better questions give you better answers. I truly believe this. That question allowed me to move out of comparison and into design. It made me think about what someone would actually need to know, what they'd need access to, what the steps are when they're written down instead of living only in your head. It turns a vague dependency into a documented process.

It's also THE question behind every system worth building in a service business. Not who else could do this, but what would it take. When you start there, you stop being the answer to every problem and start being the person who builds the answer instead, and that's a much better place to run a business from.

In My Stack

If you want a head start, copy this prompt into Claude or ChatGPT and let it walk you through the whole thing. It will ask you questions, you answer them, and it builds the document for you.

You are a business systems coach helping a service-based business owner document one repeatable task so someone else could do it without asking a single question. Ask me to describe the task I want to get off my plate. Then help me build a simple system document with these four sections: 1. What this task is and why it matters to the business 2. What someone would need to know before starting 3. What they would need access to (tools, logins, files, contacts) 4. The steps, written in plain language, in order Keep the language simple. Do not use corporate jargon. Write as if you are explaining this to a smart, capable person who has never done this specific task before. Ask me one question at a time and build the document as we go. When we are done, give me the full document formatted and ready to copy.

What task do you keep doing yourself because you think only you can do it right, and have you ever written down what "doing it right" actually means?

One Thing This Week

Pick one task you do every week and write down what someone would need to know, what they'd need to have access to, and the steps written down for someone else to do it without asking you a single question.

Hit reply and let me know the first task you're getting off your plate.

Discretion Is the Absence of Systems

From the Floor

When I ran JCF Boot Camp in Jersey City, I had a team of coaches. Good ones. They cared about the members, they showed up on time, and they brought real energy to every session.

But every single one of them ran sessions differently. One opened the huddle with a pep talk. Another skipped it and went straight into the demo. So I built what I called the Anatomy of a JCF Session. One page. It defined the structure every coach followed:

Members warm up on their own before the session starts. Huddle at the top to set the intention and prime the session, ending with the "Better Body. Better Life." breakdown. Demo. Pre-workout. Main workout. Finisher. Cool down with announcements. Post breakdown: "All day. Everyday."

That was it. No script for what to say during the huddle, no requirement that every coach sound like me. The structure was the standard. The personality on top of it was theirs.

The moment that document existed, the member experience became recognizable no matter who was leading. Tuesdays felt like JCF. Thursdays felt like JCF. Because it wasn't the Daris way anymore. It was the JCF way.

The Anatomy of a JCF Session didn't tell coaches what to say or how to coach. It told them what the session looks like, the order of events, the bookends, the non-negotiable touchpoints. Within that structure, every coach brought their own style and their own energy, and the members got the benefit of both: a consistent experience and a human one.
